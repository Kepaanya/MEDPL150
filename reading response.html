This reading of the graphic novel, Motherhood in Crisis, brought up many emotions in me. All of the imagery and text come together to make for a compelling and engaging story. Out of the three short stories given, I chose to write about the Birth in my village story because it impacted me the most. The woman in this story is very strong for what she went through, and the graphic, accompanied by the word bubbles, added an extra layer of emotion to what the main character, Memunatu, was feeling through her birth and medical treatment. I think the use of illustrations in this story allows the reader to understand the emotions and experiences of the characters in an empathetic and sympathetic way. Through these illustrations, we can see the worry, shame, and uncertainty that Memunatu experiences during her first childbirth in her village. Instead of reading a text-only story about what she went through, we get the opportunity to visualize the situation through the detailed graphics and feel what the story conveys more. The illustrations make the experience feel more tangible and help the reader understand how difficult and traumatic this was for Memunatu. This story also features a real-life video testimonial from a nurse who aided Memunatu during her treatment because it can show emotions and experiences in a way that text or illustration cannot. Even though it was only one video in the story, it still holds a lot of power overall. There were also photographs that show real people and the medical centre from the story. These photos of the size 3 medical device, the women on the treatment beds, and Menunatu and her baby help readers understand the actual environment and conditions they experienced. In comparison, the illustrations in the first half of the story hold the same anguish Memunatu felt as the Lie-lie nurse attempted to treat her while she was in pain from her medical issues. The artist conveyed these specific details and facial expressions through detailed line work and shading of the people’s faces. The drawings also emphasize certain emotions through color and how the characters are holding themselves. This specificity makes it easier for the reader to understand the deeper context of the story rather than just the words, making the story more powerful. All in all, I think the illustrations, photos, and video in Motherhood in Crisis create an impactful interpretation of Memunatu’s experiences. Each slide in the mini graphic novel offers something unique to the story, from the detailed facial expressions captured in the drawings to the real-life happiness shown through photographs and the video testimonial from the nurse. They allow the reader to more deeply connect with the story and understand the difficulties that women like Memunatu face during childbirth in these countries. In the end, this true story illustrates how visual storytelling can communicate experiences that words and photos by themselves cannot express. With the help of real-life photos and videos in combination with digital art and graphics, the impact of the story is 10-fold more impactful for whoever reads it.

This reading by Susan Sontag has brought up many ideas for me on the subject of photography, imagery, and meaning. Capturing images has been a part of my life since I had my first iPod touch. My very own device that let me capture the world around me. An aspect of that is also how I viewed the world at that time. 10-year-old me saw no war, no recession, no political riots. At that time, I saw the world as a colorful scenery that felt like every day was summer. Perspective and photography go hand in hand when thinking about how photography is conveyed. Within Sontag’s text, I will parallel my own experiences through her examples. The first digital camera I had was a purple Nikon Coolpix. I was about 15 years old and took pictures of many things. Pictures of fruits, family, and nature. All of these things were part of my daily life experience, and I sought to capture all of it. Capturing things as I saw them helped their image live long after I experienced them. Sontag speaks about this immortalized version of images captured as something that can live beyond humans. This brings a sense of detachment between any photo I took and myself. All those photos on my camera still exist in my world as long as I acknowledge their existence. Beyond the memory card, they exist in my memory too. Feelings of detachment also have closeness and sentimental value. These pictures mean something more than words can describe. It allows for the reliving of an event that I experienced. But only the photographer and subjects can relive it exactly. The closeness and detachment of photo-taking are limited. I cannot see a photo of 9/11 and feel a sense of closeness or detachment to it because I didn't have any conscious awareness during the time of that event. There are limitations in how photography is felt and perceived, which can alter our view of reality. With cameras being everywhere at once in today's age, it speaks to the evolution of surveillance. Everything can be seen through our eyes and through a lens. Not only can people take photographs of everything that meets their eye, but they can also transform them into creatively inspired projects. Just as street photographers take pictures of buildings and people walking, I can use images I take of gum wrappers on a sidewalk, or a puddle with the right hue of green, as inspiration for artwork and illustrations. Furthermore, I can recreate the feeling of when I first saw these things and have the viewer feel the same with the help of enhancements and text. All in all, I use photography and image making as a tool to express myself. The number of images I capture in a week is equally as important as the art I make from them. Visual information in the modern world is saturated, but by adding my own sense of self, my creative trademark, I can evolve just as my works of art do.
